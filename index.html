The shadow hypothesis

What if the image
is what the sunlight
left behind?

Paint the light parts of a face onto glass. Set it above darker linen. The exposed cloth bleaches; the shadowed areas stay dark. As the sun travels, the shadows shift and overlap.[1]

Make the painting.

The shadow hypothesis gives several puzzling features a possible mechanical explanation. The artist paints highlights; light performs the transfer.

01 / The negative

No need to paint in reverse.

White paint shades the linen. The exposed background becomes lighter while the shaded face remains darker. The inversion follows from selective bleaching; the painter need not anticipate a photographic negative.

02 / An image without paint on cloth

The paint stays on the glass.

The proposed process deposits no image-forming pigment on the linen. That addresses an objection to an ordinary painted picture, though matching the actual fiber chemistry remains a separate test.

03 / Soft tones from rough marks

The sun softens the brushwork.

As the sun moves, shadows fall in slightly different places. Their accumulated exposure can turn distinct painted marks into gradual tones. The artist does not have to place every transition directly on the cloth.

04 / Apparent three-dimensional form

Tones can become a relief.

A painted face already distributes light and dark around facial features. Treating the transferred tones as heights can produce a recognizable relief. Whether those heights describe real anatomy is the harder question.

04 / The evidence

A date is one question.
An image is another.

The cloth’s age, its material, and the origin of its image ask different things of the evidence.

Keep the questions distinct.

The 1988 radiocarbon tests, published in 1989, placed the tested linen in the medieval period: 1260–1390 CE. Three laboratories participated. Objections to the sampling and interpretation remain part of the literature.[2]

In April 2026, an examination of two retained Arizona fragments found their textile characteristics consistent with the main cloth, with no signs of repair or significant contaminants in those fragments. This is a small, specific examination of sampling integrity.[3]

Neither result tells us how to make the image. Conversely, making a similar image does not tell us when the cloth was woven or establish who made it. That is the space these experiments explore.

Read the evidence notes ↗
1978

A close examination

The STURP investigation studied the image and its chemistry. Its 1981 summary reported changes in linen fibrils and no sufficient image-forming explanation. These are the team’s conclusions, with a specific experimental scope.[4]

2005

A painted window

Wilson published his sunlight experiments. He reported negative-like images and compared their height renderings, while leaving microscopic and chemical comparisons unresolved.[1]

2022

A different dating approach

An X-ray study proposed an older age under assumptions about centuries of temperature and humidity. Those environmental assumptions are essential to the inference.[5]

2025–26

New models, new samples

Digital contact models compare bodies with shallow reliefs. Recent textile and DNA studies examine surviving samples. Each answers a narrower question than the identity of the man in the image.[6][7]

A resemblance can begin an inquiry.
The useful question is what survives a test.
