Scope

Two image questions.

Can moving shadows form a softened negative on bleaching linen? What happens when photographic brightness becomes height? These labs explore those questions; they do not establish the Shroud’s age, provenance, or identity.

The sunlight lab starts with Beauchamp’s photographed glass painting. The independent brush study contains no Shroud pixels. Shroud-derived masks and fitted paintings use the target itself, so resemblance cannot independently validate the proposed origin.

The shadow hypothesis

What could light explain?

In Wilson’s proposal, paint stays on glass. The linen image comes from differences in bleaching. That offers possible answers to several objections to a painted image.

The negative mechanism follows Wilson’s account; the pigment objection comes from STURP’s summary. Blur is a prediction of this optical model. Apparent depth is tested with this site’s control images.

The unresolved material question

Wilson’s FAQ leaves chemical comparisons open. Our simulator cannot test microscopic discoloration, reverse-side effects, blood–image relationships, aging, or historical feasibility. The replication plan addresses those material comparisons.

Research sources / September 2026

What the studies establish.

[1] N. D. WILSON · 2005 · EXPERIMENTAL ACCOUNT

The shadow hypothesis

White-painted glass shaded linen under sunlight and a stationary lamp. The gallery shows the results; the FAQ addresses glass, chemistry, reverse-side images, and fading. His original article describes blurring images before comparing height renderings. These exploratory trials do not establish a material match to the Shroud.

[2] DAMON ET AL. · NATURE 337, 611–615 · 1989

Radiocarbon dating of the Shroud of Turin

Arizona, Oxford, and Zurich measured linen from one sampling location. The reported interval was 1260–1390 CE at at least 95% confidence. It dates the tested linen. Casabianca et al. (2019) reassessed the released raw data; sampling and statistical objections require their own evidence.

[3] FREER-WATERS & JULL · NPJ HERITAGE SCIENCE · APRIL 2026

Textile fragments from the 1988 samples

Two retained Arizona fragments had textile features consistent with the main cloth, with no signs of repair or significant contamination. The authors acknowledge their limited coverage. This is a textile examination, not a new date.

[4] STURP · 1978 INVESTIGATION / 1981 SUMMARY

STURP’s conclusions

The team reported image-related changes in linen fibrils and argued against paint as the explanation. It found no mechanism accounting for all its observations. Its papers document the tests; the summary does not exclude every possible artistic or natural process.

[5] DE CARO ET AL. · HERITAGE 5, 860–870 · 2022

X-ray dating of a linen sample

A cellulose-degradation model gives compatibility with a roughly 2,000-year age under assumed temperatures and humidity across thirteen centuries of unknown storage. The estimate depends on that model and environmental history.

[6] CICERO MORAES · ARCHAEOMETRY · ONLINE 2025 / 2026 ISSUE

Image formation: a digital 3D approach

Digital contact simulations found a shallow relief more compatible with photographed contours than a human model. The result depends on the geometry and contact assumptions; it does not test every formation process or identify image chemistry. Open model data ↗

[7] BARCACCIA ET AL. · SCIENTIFIC REPORTS · JULY 2026

DNA in the official 1978 sample collection

Mixed human, plant, animal, and microbial sources included a lineage matching the collector. These findings concern contamination and preservation, not the figure’s identity. Two dated reliquary threads must not be confused with newly dated image-bearing cloth.

Sunlight lab / Model 1.0

Light, shadow, exposure.

The calculation uses flat, parallel linen and ideal glass. Paint opacity ranges from 0 to 1. Face inputs assume 300 mm width; the full cloth assumes 1,100 mm. Height follows the photograph’s proportions.

Shadow position

Sun direction follows latitude, day, and local solar time using the NOAA declination approximation. Days are sampled every half hour; elevations below 5° are excluded. Top-painted glass refracts light with index 1.5. Paint beneath the glass has only air-gap displacement.

lateral shift = gap × tan(air angle)
+ glass thickness × tan(refracted angle)

sin(refracted angle) = sin(air angle) / 1.5

Displacements rotate into cloth coordinates. Five rays approximate the sun’s 0.266° angular radius; bilinear sampling handles fractional pixels. Space outside the painting is transparent.

Exposure and bleaching

A clear-sky proxy weights light by elevation and assumed atmospheric attenuation. Integrated dose is in modeled peak-sun hours, without measured weather or spectra. A uniform diffuse component fills the shadows.

E(t) = sin(elevation) × exp[−0.14 × (1/sin(elevation) − 1)]

D(x,y) = Σ E(t) Δt × [1 − (1 − diffuse) × opacity × mask(x+Δx,y+Δy)]

R(x,y) = 0.88 − 0.53 × exp[−rate × D(x,y)]

Assumed reflectance R starts at 0.35 and approaches 0.88. The default rate is 0.055 per modeled peak-sun hour. None of these values is calibrated to Wilson’s linen or the Shroud; simulated exposure times depend on them.

Controls and measurements

The stationary control is an overhead point source with the moving sun’s energy weights. Equal incident dose isolates angular distribution and movement. It does not reproduce Wilson’s lamp spectrum or intensity.

  • Shadow spread: dose-weighted RMS displacement around the mean ray position, in millimetres.
  • Tonal range: 95th minus 5th percentile reflectance, in percentage points.
  • Playback: interpolation between sixteen saved exposure states.

The schematic exaggerates spacing. The calculation uses the entered distances; display colors and weave do not affect exposure.

Inverse experiment

Finding a paint mask.

Normalized target tones are mapped to 75% of the contrast achievable between constant clear and opaque masks. The inverse bleaching law converts them to a desired shadow dose. This is a design choice, not measured Shroud reflectance.

The forward model’s ray weights form a sparse transport operator. Twenty-four projected-gradient steps use its exact adjoint and a small smoothness penalty, constraining opacity to 0–1. The solver minimizes exposure-space error; displayed RMSE measures reflectance error. Blur and finite resolution can limit the fit. No historical brushwork constraint is imposed.

Target and result share a palette. The residual shows absolute difference, with maximum brightness at errors ≥ 0.2. Resemblance is target-informed, not independent evidence of origin. The saved record includes the source checksum and conditions.

The depth claim

Relief is one step.

HISTORY’s The Real Face of Jesus? (2010) featured Ray Downing and Studio Macbeth. His biography links the film to later portraits. The reference gallery distinguishes a VP-8 relief, a project working view, and the later Jesus in Death artwork.

Schwortz’s VP-8 account describes the 1976 density-to-relief work and interprets its recognizable shape as cloth-to-body distance information. The reproduced green image comes from his 1997 demonstration with Kevin Moran.

Jackson, Jumper & Ercoline (1984) compared intensity with modeled cloth–body distance. Their broad shape correction also used a volunteer’s body as a reference (section II.D). Those added coordinates matter: the final shape did not come from image tones alone.

Downing’s explanation distinguishes cloth shape from cloth–body distance. His process account also describes separating a blood map using color and applying it to a 3D model. His workflow extends beyond grayscale-to-height conversion.

The lab opens in cloth–body mode, with direct relief available for comparison. It explores these principles, not Downing’s exact method: his linked accounts do not provide the original data or a complete numerical recipe. The grayscale inputs here also cannot reproduce his color-based blood separation.

Depth lab

Brightness becomes a surface.

In Brightness → height, linked views share the image grid, inversion, camera, height scale, and material. The left retains resampled tones; the right applies your processing.

image → resampled grayscale → optional inversion → baseline height
baseline → Gaussian smoothing → optional range stretch
→ tonal exponent → adjusted height

Grayscale weights decoded RGB by (0.2126, 0.7152, 0.0722). These are display-encoded values, not calibrated linear reflectance. “Unfiltered” means no adjustment after resampling, not raw camera data.

Smoothing uses a Gaussian with clamped edges; its standard deviation is in analysis-grid pixels, default 1.25. “Unsmoothed image” disables smoothing and range stretching so both surfaces agree. Inversion affects both. Profiles compare the fields before display-height scaling.

Stretching maps the processed range to 0–1; the exponent changes its distribution. Relief height supplies no millimetre calibration. Prior photographic processing is unknown.

Known geometry and misleading light

The synthetic face encodes its actual heights as brightness. The side-lit control uses the same geometry with directional light and a darker beard. Pearson correlation compares adjusted heights with the known reference over the full image; it is not an anatomical validation score.

The Shroud has no known reference face. Folds, stains, weave, and photographic artifacts can all become heights. Apply identical processing when comparing images; smoothing can remove evidence as well as noise.

Depth lab / Cloth–body mode

The missing cloth shape.

Intensity is interpreted as a vertical gap below a chosen cloth surface. Image coordinates stay fixed: this is a height-field model, without lateral unwrapping, cloth mechanics, or completion of hidden anatomy.

s = processed signal, 0–1; D = chosen maximum gap
Linear: gap = D × (1 − s)
Exponential: gap = −D/3 × ln[e⁻³ + s × (1 − e⁻³)]
candidate body = assumed cloth − gap

Both illustrative laws map the brightest negative tone to contact and the darkest to D. They are not measured response curves. Invert positive images first. Processing uses the same smoothing, optional min–max stretch, and exponent as direct relief. These change inferred distances.

Two ways to supply geometry

Flat or curved cloth. With u and v spanning −1 to 1 across the image, cloth height is 60 − A·u² − B·v² mm. A and B set the drop to the sides and ends; both are zero for flat cloth. The 60 mm origin only locates the surface in space. Curvature is chosen, not solved from textile mechanics.

Broad shape + image. A featureless oval cap supplies the head: H × √max(0, 1 − ((x−0.5)/0.42)² − ((y−0.47)/0.46)²), where x and y span 0–1. Cloth is then defined as that cap plus a Gaussian-smoothed gap map. Subtracting the gap leaves the cap plus finer image variations. The filter’s standard deviation is the selected percentage of image width. This deliberately exposes the supplied shape; it does not estimate cloth independently.

Scale and interpretation

The two panels share one physical scale, with no vertical exaggeration. Image width, drape, and distance range are all assumptions. The default 37 mm range is inspired by the conditional 3.7 cm background intersection discussed in Jackson et al. (1984); it is not a measured Shroud gap or a documented Downing setting.

Any cloth/body pair separated by the same gap reproduces the same processed tones under the selected law. Such a fit cannot establish a unique face or an image-formation mechanism. Folds, weave, stains, and photographic processing remain in the signal; smoothing does not identify or remove blood selectively.

A test with known geometry

A fixed schematic face is encoded beneath a known curved cloth using a linear distance law. The correct inverse settings recover it. Wrong drape, scale, or processing changes the result; RMSE measures height error over the full grid, in model millimetres, without re-alignment or rescaling. This tests numerical recovery within the model, not the historical hypothesis. An independent scan and physical imaging experiment would be the next validation.

Reproducibility

Keep the inputs and settings.

Both labs run locally in your browser. A settings link restores sunlight parameters and a built-in painting. Custom drawings need an experiment download, which stores the painting, settings, selected-frame reflectance, and model version.

Completed exposures become the depth lab’s latest sunlight result across tabs. “Examine this image in 3D” saves the selected playback frame. Without a saved result, the lab computes a labeled Beauchamp example. Inversion starts enabled so darker retained tones become higher.

Saved home-page comparisons and the default lab use the same 113 × 145 Beauchamp mask. Regenerate them with npm run results. Download the conditions and measurements or input painting. Other presets retain their own proportions and resolutions.

The JavaScript model has checks for solar geometry, refraction, equal-dose controls, bleaching, opacity, exposure, and known depth. Those verify implementation, not physical validity.
