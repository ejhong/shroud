01 / What this site asks

A useful experiment has an edge.

This site explores two image questions: whether moving shadows can produce a softened, negative-like image on a bleaching surface, and what happens when a photograph’s brightness is treated as height. Neither experiment establishes the Shroud’s age, provenance, or the identity of its figure.

The sunlight lab is inspired by N. D. Wilson’s hypothesis. Its default input is extracted from Beauchamp’s actual glass painting. An optional constructed brush study starts without Shroud pixels. Its Shroud-derived presets deliberately use the target photograph as the painting; these are image-transfer demonstrations. A close match there cannot independently validate the proposed origin.

The historical object remains richer than the models. A theory of its formation must meet evidence about material, chemistry, image distribution, history, and manufacture as well as visual resemblance.

The shadow hypothesis / The painting objections

What would the light explain?

An objection to pigment painted onto linen does not automatically exclude every process involving an artist. In Wilson’s proposal, the artistic input is on glass and the final image comes from differences in bleaching. That changes which constraints the process must meet.

The puzzleThe proposed explanationThe remaining test
A photographic negative without knowledge of photographyLight paint shelters dark linen; uncovered linen lightens. The bright parts of the painting become the darker parts of the cloth.Measure the actual tonal response and image contrast in replicated linen exposures.
An image without an image-forming pigment layerPaint controls the shadow from a separate pane. No paint needs to be deposited onto the cloth.Compare image and background fibers chemically and microscopically. An absence of paint alone does not establish selective sun bleaching.
Soft transitions despite a roughly painted sourceMoving shadows integrate exposure over neighboring positions. In our model, this can soften the painted marks.Compare edge widths and fine detail with calibrated photographs. Too much blur destroys the face as well as the brushwork.

Sun movement does not automatically encode true anatomical depth.

The negative-image mechanism follows Wilson’s account. The pigment objection comes from STURP’s 1981 summary. The blur explanation is a prediction of the optical model here; the depth discussion is an interpretation tested by this site’s control images. These are distinct levels of evidence.

What is still open?

Wilson’s FAQ acknowledges unresolved chemical comparisons and proposes further physical work. The present simulator cannot test the microscopic depth or distribution of discoloration, image and background fiber chemistry, reverse-side effects, blood–image relationships, or long-term aging. It also does not establish that the required glass, materials, and workflow were used historically. A visual mechanism can address some “impossible to paint” arguments while leaving these material and historical questions open.

See the published physical results, then run the extracted painting through the simulator.

02 / Evidence notes · September 2026

Sources, with their scope attached.

[1] N. D. WILSON · 2005 · EXPERIMENTAL ACCOUNT

The shadow hypothesis

Wilson reports using white-painted glass to shade linen during sunlight bleaching, comparing differently oriented exposures with a stationary lamp. His image gallery documents the painting and results. The full FAQ discusses objections, including glass, fiber chemistry, the reverse surface, and fading. His original article describes blurring images before comparing height renderings. The account is a proposed mechanism and exploratory demonstration, not a validated match to every material property of the Shroud.

[2] DAMON ET AL. · NATURE 337, 611–615 · 1989

Radiocarbon dating of the Shroud of Turin

Samples were measured by laboratories in Arizona, Oxford, and Zurich. The reported calendar interval was 1260–1390 CE at at least 95% confidence. This dates the tested linen material. Questions about sampling and later statistical reanalyses should be considered on their own evidence; an unexplained image does not itself invalidate a carbon measurement.

For a subsequent reassessment of the released data, see Casabianca et al. (2019), “Radiocarbon Dating of the Turin Shroud: New Evidence from Raw Data.”

[3] FREER-WATERS & JULL · NPJ HERITAGE SCIENCE · 18 APRIL 2026

Analysis of textile fragments from the 1988 radiocarbon samples

The authors examined two retained Arizona fragments, reporting textile features consistent with the main cloth and no signs of repair or significant contamination in those fragments. They acknowledge that these small samples may not represent every portion of the cloth. This is a microscopy and textile study, not a new dating of the complete Shroud or a test of image formation.

[4] SHROUD OF TURIN RESEARCH PROJECT · 1978 INVESTIGATION / 1981 SUMMARY

STURP’s published conclusions

The team reported image-related changes in linen fibrils and argued against paint as the image-forming explanation. It did not establish a mechanism accounting for all its observations. The linked text is the team’s historical summary; it is not a declaration that every possible artistic or natural process has been experimentally excluded. The published papers are the more detailed record.

[5] DE CARO ET AL. · HERITAGE 5, 860–870 · 2022

X-ray dating of a Turin Shroud linen sample

The authors use structural degradation of cellulose to infer age. Their compatibility with a roughly 2,000-year age depends on assumptions about average temperature and humidity over thirteen centuries of unknown storage history. The inference is conditional on that aging model and environmental history. It is not an independent, assumption-free calendar date.

[6] CICERO MORAES · ARCHAEOMETRY · ONLINE 28 JULY 2025 / 2026 ISSUE

Image formation: a digital 3D approach

Digital cloth/contact simulations compare a human model with a shallow relief. The author finds the relief’s contact pattern more compatible with the photographed contours. This result concerns those geometry and contact assumptions. It does not test every non-contact formation process, or identify the chemistry of the actual image. The study’s data are available.

[7] BARCACCIA ET AL. · SCIENTIFIC REPORTS · 9 JULY 2026

DNA signatures in the official 1978 sample collection

The analyses report multiple human, plant, animal, and microbial sources, including a lineage matching the sample collector. Such mixtures illuminate contamination and preservation. They do not identify the individual represented by the image. The paper also dates two threads collected from the reliquary, which should not be confused with new dates for the image-bearing cloth.

03 / Experiment 01 · Model 1.0

From light to a tonal image.

The model uses a horizontal, flat linen plane and a parallel, ideal glass pane. Paint has an effective opacity from 0 to 1. Light directions follow latitude, day of year, and local solar time. The face presets assume a 300 mm image width; the full-cloth preset uses 1,100 mm. Height follows the input’s pixel aspect ratio.

Where the shadow falls

Solar declination uses the NOAA fractional-year approximation. Each day is sampled at half-hour intervals. Contributions below 5° solar elevation are excluded. Top-painted glass uses Snell’s law with refractive index 1.5. For paint on the lower face, only the air-gap displacement remains.

lateral shift = gap × tan(air angle)
+ glass thickness × tan(refracted angle)

sin(refracted angle) = sin(air angle) / 1.5

The displacement is rotated into cloth coordinates. Five rays approximate the sun’s angular disc, with a 0.266° angular radius. Bilinear sampling integrates fractional-pixel shadows. Beyond the painted rectangle the model assumes transparent space.

How exposure accumulates

A clear-sky proxy weights each sample by solar elevation and an assumed atmospheric attenuation. Its irradiance is one for an overhead sun. Integrating it gives modeled peak-sun hours, rather than measured weather or spectral energy. Uniform diffuse light fills part of every shadow.

E(t) = sin(elevation) × exp[−0.14 × (1/sin(elevation) − 1)]

D(x,y) = Σ E(t) Δt × [1 − (1 − diffuse) × opacity × mask(x+Δx,y+Δy)]

R(x,y) = 0.88 − 0.53 × exp[−rate × D(x,y)]

R is an assumed reflectance, starting at 0.35 and tending toward 0.88. The default rate is 0.055 per modeled peak-sun hour. These values make a controllable demonstration. They have not been measured for Wilson’s linen or the Shroud. Changing the rate can change the simulated number of days required substantially.

Controls and measurements

The stationary control is an ideal overhead point source. It retains every energy weight from the moving-sun exposure, making the total incident dose identical. This isolates the effect of angular distribution and movement. It is not a calibrated reconstruction of Wilson’s sunlamp spectrum or intensity.

Shadow spread is the dose-weighted root-mean-square displacement about the mean ray position, in millimetres. It measures the modeled path’s spread, not the historical Shroud’s blur. Tonal range is the final image’s 95th minus 5th percentile reflectance, in percentage points. A high value is not a score for historical accuracy.

Playback interpolates between sixteen saved exposure states. The schematic’s plane spacing and image placement are illustrative. The calculation uses the numerical distances. Cloth colors and woven texture are display choices; neither is fed back into exposure.

A real-material follow-up would measure unpainted and shaded linen at several doses, with repeat swatches, controlled moisture, and calibrated photography. Microscopy and chemical comparisons would then test properties this optical model cannot determine.

Working backward from a photograph.

The optional mask search is deliberately informed by its target. Normalized photographic tones are mapped to 75% of the achievable contrast between fully clear and fully opaque constant masks under the chosen exposure. Inverting the assumed bleaching law converts that target to a desired shadow dose. This tonal mapping is a design choice, not a reflectance measurement of the original cloth.

The same ray weights used in the forward calculation form a sparse transport operator. Twenty-four projected-gradient steps, using its exact adjoint and a small smoothness penalty, adjust the paint. Each step constrains opacity values to 0–1. The solver minimizes exposure-space error; the reported before/after RMSE is evaluated in modeled reflectance. At large gaps, finite resolution and blur may prevent a close fit. No historical brushwork constraint is imposed.

The three comparison panels show the target and result with the same linen palette, plus their absolute difference. Brightest residual colors indicate reflectance errors of at least 0.2. Fitting the target demonstrates conditional reconstruction, not independent confirmation of the mechanism. The saved reconstruction record includes the source checksum and chosen conditions.

The motivation for the depth lab

What does “3D information” mean?

HISTORY’s The Real Face of Jesus? (2010) featured Ray Downing and Studio Macbeth’s reconstruction from the Shroud. His biography connects the television work with later published portraits. The depth lab’s gallery shows an earlier VP-8 example, a working image from Downing’s project, and his later Jesus in Death portrait. These are published reference images, not results calculated by this site.

A relief, a distance hypothesis, a reconstruction

Barrie Schwortz’s VP-8 account describes the 1976 use of a density-to-relief instrument on a Shroud photograph. He interprets its recognizable relief as evidence of cloth-to-body distance encoding. The green image reproduced here comes from his separate 1997 demonstration with Kevin Moran, using prints from Schwortz’s own negatives.

The underlying research is more specific than a striking picture. Jackson, Jumper & Ercoline (1984), Applied Optics 23, 2244–2270, studied whether image intensity could be related to distances between a body shape and a draping cloth by one mapping function. They reported consistency under their analysis. That is a model-dependent relationship to examine, rather than a direct measurement of an unknown person’s facial coordinates.

Downing’s explanation likewise distinguishes cloth shape from inferred cloth-to-body distance. A reconstruction must relate that proposed distance field to the cloth’s drape and the body’s geometry. In another account of his process, he describes using color differences in high-definition photographs to separate a blood map and apply it to a 3D model. His published workflow therefore includes operations beyond a single grayscale-to-height conversion.

Our interpretation: a rendered portrait also presents choices about surface appearance and lighting that a scalar brightness map alone cannot determine. This lab reproduces neither Downing’s complete reconstruction nor the original VP-8 signal chain. It isolates one testable step: what relief results from a specified photograph and an explicit tonal mapping?

What a useful comparison would need

A strict replication would need the original scan, crop, calibration, processing steps, and geometry assumptions. Those are not established for the supplied input. A rough-looking result here cannot alone refute the published studies; a smooth or recognizable one cannot establish anatomical accuracy, identity, or a unique image-formation mechanism. Known-depth and side-lit controls help separate the behavior of this tool from claims about the historical object.

04 / Experiment 02

From brightness to a surface.

The supplied Python experiment maps grayscale brightness to height. This browser version preserves the photograph’s rectangular proportions, separates smoothing from height scale, and exposes the tonal choices. The source image remains visible. Linked views compare the unfiltered brightness surface with the adjusted surface, using identical cameras, relief scales, and materials.

image → resampled grayscale → optional inversion → baseline height
baseline → Gaussian smoothing → optional range stretch
→ tonal exponent → adjusted height

Grayscale uses weighted RGB values (0.2126, 0.7152, 0.0722) from decoded images. These are display-encoded pixel values, not calibrated linear-light reflectance. Inputs are resampled for the interactive analysis. Smoothing is a Gaussian standard deviation in analysis-grid pixels, with clamped edges. The default is 1.25 pixels; “Unsmoothed image” sets it to zero and disables range stretching, so the two surfaces agree. “Unfiltered” means no additional smoothing or tonal adjustment after resampling; it does not mean raw camera data. Inversion applies to both views. Horizontal and vertical sections overlay both fields before display height scaling.

Range stretching maps the darkest and lightest processed values to 0 and 1. The tonal exponent then adjusts their distribution. The relief-height control exaggerates or flattens the surface; it supplies no physical depth calibration. A scan’s prior photographic processing is unknown.

What the controls check

The known-depth control begins with an analytic, schematic face. Its brightness values are its actual heights, providing a positive control. The side-lit control renders the same geometry with directional illumination and darker beard reflectance. The displayed Pearson correlation compares inferred heights with the known reference, over the full rectangular field. It checks this synthetic example; it is not an anatomical validation metric.

For the Shroud, there is no known reference face geometry. Creases, stains, fiber structure, and photographic processing can all become heights. Smoothing may make a surface easier to recognize while also removing evidence. The same operations must be applied when comparing images.

05 / Keep the experiment

Repeat it. Change one thing.

Both labs work in a modern browser without a server-side calculation or account. Uploaded images and drawings remain local. A settings link restores the sunlight parameters and a built-in painting; it cannot include a custom drawing. An experiment download contains the full painting array, settings, reflectance at the selected playback point, and model version.

Completed sunlight exposures are saved in this browser and become the depth lab’s latest sunlight experiment, including in another tab. “Examine this image in 3D” also saves the selected playback frame. With no saved result, that source loads a clearly labeled example made with Beauchamp’s painting. The depth lab starts with inversion enabled so the darker retained image becomes higher. Further display processing is visible in its controls.

The home page’s saved comparisons use the 113 × 145 opacity mask extracted from the photograph of Beauchamp’s glass painting. They can be regenerated with npm run results. Their numerical settings and measurements are in experiments.json; the input painting is available too. The default live experiment uses the same mask and analysis grid. Other image presets have their own aspect ratios and resolutions.

The complete numerical model is readable JavaScript. Its checks cover solar geometry, refraction, matched-dose controls, uniform bleaching, opacity, monotonic exposure, and known-depth comparisons. These test the implementation; physical validation remains a separate task.
