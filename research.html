A research agenda / Five projects worth funding

What would change
our understanding?

A beautiful image invites a theory. A useful experiment gives that theory something difficult to explain.

Five priorities across optics, imaging, chemistry, textile history, radiocarbon dating, and genetics. Each starts with a question, specifies the measurements, and says what the result could actually settle.

The Shadow Shroud test ↓
An editorial funding recommendation

Fund the uncertainty.

This ranking is our judgment about information gained, feasibility, and usefulness across competing explanations. It draws on selected primary literature through September 2026, including the recent textile and DNA studies; it is not an exhaustive review or a scientific consensus.

The order is a funding priority, not a timetable. Begin the sunlight pilot and the imaging audit together. The chemistry project supplies essential benchmarks for judging any reproduction. New access to the cloth or its samples depends on its custodians and conservation requirements.

01
Priority one / A direct test of Shadow Shroud

Can sunlight match
the material, too?

Wilson demonstrated an image-making process. The next investment should be a controlled comparison of the resulting linen with the Shroud’s measured properties.

Why this project: Wilson’s account reports a painted pane, moving-sun exposures, and a stationary-lamp comparison. His FAQ explicitly leaves microscopic and chemical agreement untested. That is the gap to close. Historical studies provide reflectance, fluorescence, and fiber observations to test against, with their sampling limits retained. [1] [2] [3]

Our assessment

The shadow mechanism is worth a controlled pilot: it offers a straightforward route to an inverted, softened image. Its credibility as an explanation of the Shroud depends on whether one reproducible process also matches the material evidence. The browser simulator currently assumes bleaching behavior; it does not establish that match.

A
Optical transport

Measure the light.

First expose simple stripes, edges, and gray steps through measured glass. Vary pane–cloth gap and orientation. Record spectral irradiance at the linen, glass transmission, paint opacity, temperature, humidity, and time.

Compare moving illumination with stationary illumination at matched total dose and comparable spectra. Include clear glass, an opaque shield, and no-glass controls. Predict the edge spread from geometry before exposing a new pattern.

Decision: Can measured optics predict contrast and blur on an unseen exposure, within uncertainty?
B
Linen photochemistry

Measure the fibers.

Use a documented range of unbleached linens and surface preparations, with untreated and dark-stored controls from each batch. Any artificial aging is a separate experimental variable. Measure before and after exposure, in both sheltered and illuminated regions.

Compare reflectance spectra, fluorescence, chemical markers, distribution of colored fibrils, and color depth through threads and individual fibers. Measure both faces of the fabric. Distinguish bleaching of a coating from alteration of cellulose.

Decision: Can the same material and exposure match the image–background differences, including where the color resides?
C
Independent replication

Freeze the recipe.

Choose the recipe using pilot data, then have a second laboratory repeat it with coded specimens and independent analysis. Register endpoints and acceptance ranges before seeing the replication results.

Include Beauchamp’s design for historical continuity and a painting made without tracing the Shroud. Keep target-derived masks as transfer demonstrations. Compare with contact and thermal reference samples using the same measurements.

Decision: Does one frozen recipe reproduce the optical and material signature together, across independent batches?

Replication unit: an independently prepared and exposed swatch, not a pixel or an individual fiber from one swatch. Randomize positions and batch order. Use the pilot’s between-swatch variation to set a confirmatory sample size; report every exposure, including failures.

A negative result rejects the specified recipe and parameter range. It does not rule out every imaginable light-based process. A positive result establishes consistency with the tested evidence, not historical authorship.

First funded deliverable

An open pilot dataset: linen provenance, optical measurements, exposure logs, spectra, microscopy, uncertainty estimates, and a preregistered plan for the independent trial.

02
Photometry / geometry / computer vision

Make an image
we can measure.

Build a calibrated image atlas and a fair benchmark for the “3D information” claim.

Why this project: the 1982 photography report distinguished film density from cloth reflectance. The 1984 distance study modeled a body beneath draped cloth. Later contact simulations explore other geometry assumptions. A random online scan cannot stand in for all of these measurements. [4] [5] [6]

Run the experiment.

Audit original negatives, digital files, acquisition records, and rights first. Where new access is possible, acquire registered images under measured illumination, with radiometric targets, resolution tests, and repeat exposures. Map weave, folds, stains, repairs, and uncertain regions; preserve raw files alongside every derived layer.

Test the claim.

Measure known 3D forms and their draped cloths, then photograph controlled contact images, paintings, and shadow transfers. Fix processing on training examples; evaluate withheld shapes and regions. Compare direct brightness relief with explicit cloth–body models, reporting errors against the known geometry and sensitivity to crop, contrast, and drape.

What would change?

If several methods produce similar reliefs under identical processing, a face-like rendering is weak evidence for a unique origin. Reliable recovery of withheld geometry would support a particular method on those controls. The Shroud still has no known original face against which to score anatomical accuracy.

First funded deliverable

A documented archive inventory, a calibration plan, and an open control dataset with known geometry. New cloth imaging is a separate access-dependent phase.

03
Microscopy / analytical chemistry / conservation

Find exactly
what is colored.

Establish a reproducible material signature before deciding which process reproduces it.

Why this project: Heller and Adler reported chemical changes in image-bearing cellulose and argued that pigment did not account for the image. The spectral work separately measured image, background, scorch, and stain regions. These are specific historical analyses, not a substitute for a modern, independently checked sample map. [3] [2]

Run the experiment.

Inventory existing documented fibers before seeking new samples. Code image, nearby background, scorch, stain, adhesive, and mounting controls for blind analysis. Combine microscopy and microspectroscopy, validating spatial resolution, detection limits, and instrument effects on expendable linen first.

Measure the distinction.

Determine whether coloration belongs to cellulose, a surface layer, particles, or a mixture. Quantify its depth and distribution: across the weave, around individual fibers, and within them. Treat these as different scales. Compare pigments, heated linen, chemical modifications, and the sunlight specimens without identifying them to the analysts.

What would change?

A repeatable signature could exclude reproductions with the wrong material or depth distribution. Unexpected coatings or image-correlated particles would revise constraints on artistic mechanisms. Failure to detect a substance supplies a detection limit, not proof that none exists. Old tape samples may be selective and cannot describe every part of the cloth.

First funded deliverable

A provenance-checked sample inventory and an interlaboratory method comparison on coded modern controls, followed by the smallest justified historical-sample study.

04
Radiocarbon / textile history / statistics

Date a mapped textile,
with the sampling visible.

Test spatial consistency and sample history together, rather than asking one small fragment to represent everything.

Why this project: the 1989 report dated the sampled linen to 1260–1390 CE at at least 95% confidence. A 2019 reanalysis questioned data homogeneity. In 2026, examination of two retained Arizona fragments reported no repair or significant contamination, while acknowledging their limited coverage. That is reason for a carefully mapped test, not a presumption that the medieval result is wrong. [7] [8] [9]

Run the experiment.

Conservators and textile historians map weave, seams, repairs, damage, and sampling provenance. First validate preparation methods on known-age linen. If custodians authorize new sampling, select several justified locations and split sufficiently sized, documented samples among independent AMS laboratories, alongside blanks and coded known-age controls.

Measure the distinction.

Publish sample masses, preparation yields, contamination checks, replicate measurements, locations, and calibrated age distributions. Plan the statistical comparison before results arrive: distinguish location variation, laboratory offsets, and preparation effects. Analyze suspected repairs separately rather than averaging them into a single date.

What would change?

Concordant medieval dates from representative locations would strengthen the existing result. Reproducible differences would require a location-specific explanation. An older thread would need secure attribution to the main textile. Radiocarbon dates the carbon in the sampled material, not the moment its image formed or the identity depicted.

First funded deliverable

A conservation-led sampling feasibility study and blinded preparation trial. Fund new historical measurements only with authorized, sufficient material and a published analysis plan.

05
Ancient DNA / metagenomics / sample provenance

Separate biology
from handling history.

Ask whether any reproducible old signal can be distinguished from the many later contacts.

Why this project: the July 2026 study found multiple biological sources and a dominant human lineage matching the 1978 sample collector. Its damage analysis also showed substantial modern contamination. More sequencing alone does not solve attribution. [10]

Run the experiment.

Start with an independent reanalysis of available sequence data and sample records. Require extraction and library blanks, reagent controls, spatial provenance, and repeat extractions in separate ancient-DNA laboratories for any new work. Use appropriately consented handler comparisons where they can resolve contamination.

Measure the distinction.

Estimate mixtures, fragment lengths, terminal damage, coverage, and mapping uncertainty. Test whether a candidate signal recurs in independently documented samples and survives contamination models. Use the pilot to decide whether new sampling would add information. Share reproducible analysis while protecting identifiable contemporary donor data.

What would change?

An independently authenticated older signal could inform contact or preservation history. Damage alone is not a calendar date; a geographic lineage does not uniquely trace the cloth’s travels. Without a securely attributable original donor or a reference genome, this cannot identify Jesus or the person represented by the image.

First funded deliverable

A contamination audit and an explicit go/no-go recommendation for further sampling. This ranks below direct material and imaging work because attribution may remain unresolved even with more reads.

One standard across all five

A result that
someone else can check.

Register the hypothesis, comparison set, parameter limits, and decision rules before the confirmatory work. Estimate uncertainty and the smallest meaningful effect. Preserve chain of custody, calibration, raw measurements, analysis code, and negative results.

AI can help register images, trace documented processing, and compare predictions. Validate it on known controls. Generated faces, enhanced fibers, and inferred detail are illustrations or hypotheses; they are not new measurements of the cloth.

Where measurements are unavailable, the honest result is “not yet testable.” Where one recipe fails, specify which one. Where several explanations survive, design the next experiment to separate them.
